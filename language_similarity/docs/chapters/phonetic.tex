\section{Phonetic Similarity}

The phonetic similarity of languages refers to the sound patterns and pronunciation features that characterize speech. This metric reveals deeper linguistic relationships that may not be apparent from orthographic analysis alone. Languages that share similar phonetic structures often indicate common ancestry, historical contact, or parallel phonological evolution. Phonetic analysis is particularly useful for understanding the Austronesian language family, where phonics have been important to reconstructing proto-languages and tracing migration patterns~\cite{Blust_1991}.

Unlike orthographic similarity, which measures written forms, phonetic similarity shows the acoustic and articulatory properties of language. This distinction is important for Philippine languages, many of which adopted the Latin script during Spanish colonization despite maintaining distinct phonological sets. Two languages may share similar orthographies due to external influences, yet preserve divergent phonetic systems rooted in their indigenous sound structures. Conversely, languages with different spelling conventions may exhibit notable phonetic similarity, reflecting their shared Austronesian heritage.

\subsection{Methodology}

To compute the phonetic similarity between the selected Philippine languages, we employed a two-stage transformation process. First, we extracted trigrams from each word in the corpora, as described in the orthographic analysis. These trigrams were then converted to phonetic representations using the Double Metaphone algorithm. The Double Metaphone algorithm is a phonetic encoding scheme that generates two phonetic codes (primary and alternate) for each input string, accounting for variant pronunciations and phonological ambiguities common in multilingual settings.

Double Metaphone was selected over simpler encoders such as Soundex because it
handles non-English phonological patterns more robustly and produces multiple
encodings where pronunciation is ambiguous. For each trigram we obtained both the
primary and the alternate code, aggregating these into a frequency distribution
per language, which preserves the distributional properties of phonetic patterns
while abstracting away from orthographic representation.

Pairwise similarity between the resulting frequency vectors was computed using
cosine similarity, which measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors in
a multi-dimensional space and is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{cos}(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i B_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} B_i^2}}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are the phonetic frequency vectors for two languages. Cosine similarity is particularly appropriate for this analysis because it accounts for the relative distribution of phonetic features rather 
than their absolute frequencies, making it robust to variations in the corpus size.

We constructed a phonetic similarity matrix for all language pairs, with values ranging from 0 (no phonetic overlap) 
to 1 (identical phonetic distributions). The resulting matrix is visualized as a heatmap in \cref{fig:phonetic_sim_matrix}.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

The phonetic similarity matrix in \cref{fig:phonetic_sim_matrix} occupies a
markedly narrower band than its orthographic counterpart, running from 0.7622 to
0.9927 with most pairs above 0.80. Despite the divergent writing systems these
languages acquired during Spanish colonisation, their sound systems have
remained close, which is the first indication that phonology preserves the
shared Austronesian inheritance that orthography obscures.

The Bisayan languages are again the most cohesive group, and more emphatically
so. Hiligaynon and Cebuano reach 0.9927, the highest value in the matrix, and
Kinaray-a holds to both (0.9582 and 0.9650). Waray-Waray, separated by the Samar
Sea, still reaches 0.9550 with Kinaray-a: the narrow straits of the Visayas have
evidently carried contact rather than blocked it. Romblomanon again occupies the
centre, exceeding 0.97 against Tagalog (0.9716), Hiligaynon (0.9734), and
Cebuano (0.9708), consistent with Romblon's position astride the northern and
southern migration routes. Bikol and Masbatenyo are closer still at 0.9857.

Chavacano is once more the outlier, and for a reason the phonetic data makes
sharper than the orthographic. Its lowest scores, 0.7972 with Waray-Waray and
0.7881 with Tagalog, sit at the floor of the matrix. Although it borrowed
extensively from Cebuano and Tausug vocabulary, its phonotactics were reshaped
by Spanish. Tausug supplies the instructive contrast: the southernmost language
in the sample, remote from the rest and subject to maritime Southeast Asian
influence, it nonetheless remains phonetically central (0.9587 with Pangasinan,
0.9538 with Masbatenyo, 0.9519 with Bikol). Distance and contact alone therefore
do not restructure a sound system; creolisation does.

Northern Luzon forms a distinct phonological zone. Ilocano is among the lowest
non-creole languages against the central varieties (0.7622 with Hiligaynon,
0.7896 with Cebuano, 0.7936 with Tagalog) yet strong against its northern
neighbours Paranan (0.9594) and Pangasinan (0.9186), a division that follows the
Cordillera. Paranan, isolated in the Sierra Madre, is closest of all to
Pangasinan (0.9677) and unexpectedly close to Tausug (0.9435). Such long-range
affinities may indicate conservative features retained from an earlier stage and
since lost in more innovative central varieties, or independent but convergent
simplification in isolated communities; the present data cannot distinguish
these. Central Luzon coheres more weakly than the Visayas, with Tagalog reaching
0.9292 with Kapampangan and 0.8986 with Pangasinan, and Kapampangan is closer to
the distant Kinaray-a (0.9456) and Waray-Waray (0.9206) than to neighbouring
Ilocano (0.8483), which is suggestive of lowland trade networks bypassing the
highlands.

Set against the geographic distribution in \cref{fig:lingmap_out}, the Bisayan
cohesion and Ilocano's separation behind the Cordillera both track terrain
closely. The Paranan--Pangasinan affinity and Chavacano's divergence do not.
Phonetic similarity is therefore not reducible to proximity, and the languages
that most resist geographic explanation, particularly Yami and Paranan, are the
ones that would repay closer study.
