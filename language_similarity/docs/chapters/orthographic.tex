\section{Orthographic Similarity}

The orthographic similarity of languages refers to the spelling convention of a certain language. This metric is 
important because it can give insights into the historical and cultural relationships between languages. Languages 
that share similar orthographic systems may have been influenced by the same writing systems or may have had 
significant contact in the past. This gives us valuable insights as to how languages have evolved over time and how they
are related to one another.

\subsection{Methodology}

To compute the orthographic similarity between the selected Philippine languages
we used the Jaccard similarity coefficient, a measure of the overlap between two
sample sets defined as the size of their intersection divided by the size of
their union:

\begin{equation}
    J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}
\end{equation}

where \(A\) and \(B\) are the sets of unique trigrams occurring in the
orthography of two languages. We extracted these sets from the corpus of each
language, computed the coefficient for every pair, and stored the values in the
similarity matrix presented in \cref{fig:ortho_sim_matrix}.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

The orthographic similarity matrix in \cref{fig:ortho_sim_matrix} spans a wide
range, from 0.3423 to 0.9391, and that spread is itself the primary result:
adoption of the Latin script across Philippine languages has followed markedly
different paths, shaped by distinct standardisation processes, missionary
influences, and local literacy traditions. Geographic or genetic proximity does
not guarantee orthographic proximity.

The Bisayan languages form the matrix's most cohesive cluster. Hiligaynon and
Cebuano reach 0.9391, and Kinaray-a holds to both (0.9067 and 0.8805).
Romblomanon sits at the centre of this group rather than at its edge, scoring
0.9242 with Hiligaynon, 0.9143 with Kinaray-a, and 0.8870 with Cebuano while
still reaching 0.8666 with Tagalog. Its position between Luzon and the Visayas
is reflected in a writing system that resembles both. Bikol and Masbatenyo form
a second tight pair at 0.9206, consistent with their adjacency in the Bicol
Peninsula. Tagalog, for its part, pairs closely with Cebuano (0.8877) and
Hiligaynon (0.8811), a similarity plausibly sustained by education and media
between the two most widely spoken languages of the archipelago, but reaches
only 0.7985 with its Central Luzon neighbour Kapampangan.

Chavacano is the clear outlier. Its best score is 0.5228 with Pangasinan, and
most of its pairings fall between 0.35 and 0.45. As a Spanish-based creole it
inherits letter combinations and conventions from Spanish, producing a written
form unlike that of any Austronesian neighbour. That it retains any similarity
at all is attributable to the common Latin script.

The northern languages are distinct as a group but not uniform within it.
Ilocano's similarity to the central languages is low (0.5583 with Hiligaynon,
0.5588 with Cebuano, 0.5669 with Tagalog), rising only for Paranan (0.7235) and
Pangasinan (0.6818). Paranan and Yami are likewise moderate throughout, peaking
against Pangasinan (0.7828 and 0.7769), and Kapampangan is more isolated still,
reaching 0.7033 with Cebuano and only 0.5421 with Ilocano despite a long
literary history in Pampanga. Pangasinan recurs in each of these profiles, and
its own scores are unusually even, from 0.7828 with Paranan to 0.7926 with Bikol
and 0.7906 with Masbatenyo, which positions it as an orthographic middle ground
between northern and southern conventions. Tausug is comparably moderate
(0.59--0.77, peaking at 0.7762 with Bikol), notwithstanding its distance from
the rest of the sample and its historical connection to Arabic script
traditions.

Compared with the geographic distribution in \cref{fig:lingmap_out}, geography
explains these patterns only partially. The Bisayan cluster coheres and the
northern languages group loosely, but the correspondence is not absolute, and it
should not be expected to be: orthography is the most standardisable layer of a
language, and missionary, educational, and administrative decisions cut freely
across terrain. What the matrix records is therefore less a map of where these
communities live than of which of them learned to write together.
