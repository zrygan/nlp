\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Philippines is an archipelago of over 7,600 islands, comprising three main
regions: Luzon, the Visayas, and Mindanao. The country's mountainous terrain
and inter-island sea channels have historically restricted and directed contact
among language communities~\cite{Boquet_2017}. While linguists have documented
over 180 indigenous languages in the country~\cite{ethnologue2025},
quantitative measurements of their cross-linguistic similarities remain rare.

This paper quantifies the orthographic and phonetic relationships among 16
Philippine languages (listed in~\cref{tab:ph_languages}). While traditional
Austronesian linguistics maps historical divergence using lexical comparisons,
such as cognate counts in standard word lists, we propose a corpus-based
approach. By extracting $n$-gram frequencies from a biblical parallel corpus,
we measure structural and acoustic patterns without relying on direct word
meanings. Because the underlying source material is identical across all
translations, variations in $n$-gram frequencies reflect genuine linguistic
differences rather than topic bias. We then test these linguistic matrices
against geographic distances using a Mantel test to model how physical barriers
affect language variation.

Analyzing these languages requires distinguishing between orthographic and
phonetic layers. Orthographic similarity reflects spelling conventions,
revealing which languages were standardized under shared missionary,
educational, or administrative influences. On the other hand, phonetic
similarity is a more conservative measure of sound patterns and pronunciation.
It serves as a stronger indicator of genetic relationships because it is not
filtered through the recent imposition of the Latin script~\cite{Blust_1991}.
This distinction is particularly critical for Philippine languages. Many
adopted the Latin script during Spanish colonization while retaining distinct
indigenous phonological systems. Consequently, two languages might share
spelling conventions due to colonial history, or differ in spelling while
remaining phonetically close which reveals a shared Austronesian ancestry that
orthography obscures.

Geography serves as the third axis of comparison, providing the ecological
context for these linguistic divergences. Much of the country's diversity is
attributed to its fragmented topography, where mountains, rivers, and ocean
channels have acted simultaneously as connectors and
separators~\cite{Boquet_2017}. Historically, the \emph{barangay}, the smallest
socio-political unit of pre-colonial society, was typically sited near rivers
or coasts. While this supported trade, it also created pockets of linguistic
autonomy where neighboring islands could remain mutually unintelligible. This
aligns with Blust's Austronesian dispersal hypothesis~\cite{Blust_1991}, which
argues that a single protolanguage diverged as localized adaptation and
restricted contact allowed isolated communities to accumulate unique
phonological and lexical innovations over time.

The 16 languages examined in this study range from dominant regional lingua
francas to endangered local varieties, spanning the length of the archipelago
and beyond, see \cref{fig:lingmap_out}. In the northern plains of Central
Luzon, we examine Tagalog, Kapampangan, and Pangasinan. Further north, the
dataset includes coastal Ilocano, Adasen in the Cordillera interior, and
Paranan on the Sierra Madre coast. The Visayas are represented by a contiguous
cluster of Bisayan languages separated by narrow sea channels: Cebuano,
Hiligaynon, Kinaray-a, and Waray-Waray. With Bikol, Masbatenyo, and Romblomanon
occupying the transitional zone between the Visayas and Luzon. In the south, we
analyze Tausug which is spoken in the Sulu archipelago. And, Chavacano a
Spanish creole in Zamboanga. Finally, Yami, spoken on Orchid Island, Taiwan, is
included as a northern Austronesian outgroup.
